OBJECTIVE 104.1Understand basic electron theory

Atoms and charges

  • All matter is composed of molecules. Molecules are made of different atoms. 104-2
  • Each atom has a nucleus (protons and neutrons) with electrons orbiting it. 104-2
  • Electron = negative (−). Proton = positive (+). Neutron = no charge. An atom usually has equal numbers of electrons and protons. 104-2
  • Bound electrons are held in orbit by the protons’ equal attraction. Free electrons are in the outermost orbit and are held loosely. 104-2
  • An external force knocks free electrons out of orbit: chemical action, friction, or a magnetic field. The empty spot left behind is a hole. 104-2
  • Magnetism is the basis of electricity. Opposite charges attract. Like charges repel. 104-3
  • Hard steel makes a good permanent magnet (hard to magnetize and demagnetize). Soft iron is easily magnetized and demagnetized. 104-3 note

Current

  • Current is the flow of electrons from one atom to another. It makes all electrical equipment operate. 104-3
  • Letter I (for intensity). Unit amperes (A). Measured with an ammeter. 104-3
  • 1 amp = 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons passing a point in one second. 104-3
  • Electrons leave the negative terminal, go through the wire and the load, and come back into the positive terminal. They flow whenever there is a difference in potential. Electron flow is current flow. 104-4

Voltage and voltage sources

  • Voltage is the force that makes electrons flow (electromotive force, emf). It exists because of a difference in potential between two points. 104-5
  • Letter E or V. Unit volts (V). Measured with a voltmeter. 104-5
  • Water-pipe comparison: a stronger pump means more flow, and a bigger pipe means more flow. The same is true for electrons. 104-5
  • A battery is electrochemical. The negative terminal has the excess electrons and the positive has the shortage. Symbol: two lines, and the shorter line is negative. 104-5, Fig. 104-7
  • A thermocouple is two dissimilar metals joined at one end, with the junction heated, giving a small voltage. Limited capability. Used in gas heating units, cooking equipment and water heaters. If the pilot goes out, the voltage stops, the solenoid valve closes and the gas shuts off. 104-5, 104-6
  • Most common voltage source is a generator (or alternator). Its shaft is turned by wind, hydroelectric (gravity), wave motion (wind and tides) or engines. 104-8
  • DC (direct current): a source with + and − terminals, like a battery or DC power supply. The voltage is constant. AC (alternating current): the source is a generator or transformer. 104-8

Conductors and insulators

Conductors: many free electrons

Copper, brass, silver, aluminum, platinum, gold, zinc. Copper is used most because it is relatively inexpensive and has other good physical characteristics. Conductors are classified by how many electrons can travel freely. 104-6

Insulators: very, very few free electrons

Some plastics, rubber, glass, mica, some ceramics, air. Lineman’s pliers combine steel jaws (conductor) with plastic handle covers (insulator). 104-6

  • Wet wood conducts. Plywood glue may conduct, so use insulating mats made for the purpose. 104-7
  • New leather boots with rubber soles insulate, but sweaty, dirty boots don’t, because sweat is salty. 104-7
  • Pure distilled water is a perfect insulator. Adding chlorine, salt or dirt makes it more and more conductive. Never put body parts in tap or dirty water to operate energized equipment. 104-7
  • Dust on insulated wires in a panel can carry current, so keep equipment enclosed and clean. Check what cleaning solution was used on rubber mats and gloves. 104-7
  • Static electricity is a collection of charged particles at rest, usually on insulated surfaces. It has virtually no practical use to an electrician. 104-7

OBJECTIVE 104.2Recognize characteristics of electrical circuits and current flow

  • A circuit must be complete. Any break (open drawbridge, broken filament) stops the flow of current. 104-13, Fig. 104-17
  • Circuit breaker ON = closed = a conductor with zero resistance. OFF = open = an insulator (air space) with infinite resistance, so no current flows. 104-13
  • Closed breakers have zero resistance, so they can be left off circuit diagrams. 104-14 note
  • Generators and transformers are made of coils of wire called windings. Voltage is generated in the generator windings at the power plant. 104-13
  • Transformers have two windings in one enclosure. Primary = high voltage. Secondary = low voltage. They reduce a high voltage to a useable level. The distribution transformer secondary is our voltage source. 104-14
  • 4,160 V down to 240 V is a common transformation. Other common low voltages are 120, 208, 277 and 480. 104-13, 104-14
  • Current path: transformer, main breaker, busbars, 30 A breaker, heating element, then back to the transformer winding. 104-14, Fig. 104-19
  • Circuits can be drawn many equal ways. Learn to redraw them in the form you like best. 104-14

  • Resistance is the total opposition to current flow.

OBJECTIVE 104.3Relationship between voltage, current, resistance and power

  • Georg Simon Ohm developed Ohm’s Law in 1827. It shows the relationship between current (I), voltage (E) and resistance (R). 104-16
  • In words, 1 volt of pressure forces 1 ampere through 1 ohm. 104-16
  • Current is always in amperes. Voltage is always in volts. Resistance is always in ohms. 104-16
  • Triangle: cover the value you want, and the remaining letters make the formula. 104-16, Fig. 104-21
  • The two basic formulas are I = E ÷ R and P = I × E. All the others come from these. If you know any two values, you can find the rest. 104-17

The book’s examples

  • Example 1: 10 V and 5 Ω gives I = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 A. 104-16
  • Example 2: 200 mA and 10 Ω gives E = .2 × 10 = 2 V. Using 200 instead of .2 gives the wrong answer of 2,000 V. 104-17
  • Example 3: 100 V and 5 A gives R = 100 ÷ 5 = 20 Ω. 104-17

Proportionality

  • Current is directly proportional to voltage. 10 V on 5 Ω = 2 A, and 20 V on 5 Ω = 4 A: voltage doubled, so current doubled. 104-18, 104-19
  • Current is inversely proportional to resistance. 10 V on 5 Ω = 2 A, and 10 V on 10 Ω = 1 A: resistance doubled, so current halved. 104-19

OBJECTIVE 104.4Results of using different applied voltages

  • Electrical loads don’t come with a resistance value. You derive it from the nameplate ratings. That is the fixed (“hot” or operating) resistance. 104-17
  • Fixed resistance doesn’t change, whatever voltage is applied. 104-18
  • Ratings (watts or amps with rated volts) are accurate only at the rated voltage. Less voltage than rated gives less power than rated, like a weak 10 V car battery making dim headlamps. 104-18

The method (always in this order)

  1. Find fixed R from the nameplate: R = Erated² ÷ Prated (watt rating) or R = Erated ÷ Irated (amp rating). 104-19
  2. Use the applied voltage for everything else.
  3. Current: I = Eapplied ÷ R
  4. Power: P = Eapplied² ÷ R (or I × Eapplied)
  5. Check the direction. Lower voltage means lower current and lower power. Higher voltage means higher.

Worked example from the book

A lamp is rated 25 W at 12 V and the battery puts out 13 V. 104-19
R = 12² ÷ 25 = 5.76 Ω → I = 13 ÷ 5.76 = 2.2569 A → P = 13 × 2.2569 = 29.34 W

  • Don’t round to fewer than four decimal places. Rounding the current is why the book’s three power answers differ slightly. 104-20
  • Applied voltage should not exceed the rating. For resistive loads, 5% to 10% above the rating is generally acceptable. 104-20

104 SAFETYTruck backing basics

  • Whenever possible, use a spotter. It’s best to have one outside the truck. 104-21
  • No spotter? Be your own. Get out and check clearance at the back, both sides and the top. Walk around the area and scope it out before you start. 104-21
  • Get out and check again after you’ve backed more than halfway into the space. 104-21
  • Back from the left side (your “seeing side”) if you can, and check both mirrors often. 104-21
  • A truck has 10 times as many blind spots as a car. The best advice is to avoid backing whenever you can. 104-21
  • Go slowly and creep into place. If it’s not going right, pull forward, take another look, then try again. Watch for people, and plan so you interfere with traffic as little as possible. 104-21
  • Make sure you have clearance for the top of the rig, and watch for overhangs. 104-21
  • Straight truck: turn the wheel in the direction you want the rear to go. Combination rig: follow an S curve and turn the front the opposite way first. Don’t oversteer. Back out of traffic, not into it. 104-21
  • Leaving a trailer at the dock? Chock its wheels. 104-21
